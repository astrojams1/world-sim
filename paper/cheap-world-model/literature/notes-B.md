## (b) Closest works

**3DP3 (Gothoskar et al. 2021, 2111.00312).** Representation: voxel-grid object shapes with contact planes, and a hierarchical scene graph whose edges encode flush contact between objects (poses are parametrised relative to the parent), written as a probabilistic program. Inference: bottom-up pose proposals (DBSCAN clusters on unexplained depth points combined with "24 nominal orientations ... the rotational symmetries of a cube", refined with ICP), MAP pose initialisation, then involutive MCMC over scene-graph structure; the likelihood is a depth-image renderer. Training: the shape priors are learned from ~5 synthetic images per object type, and the YCB-Video experiment initialises from DenseFusion (a trained network); however Appendix B shows an RGB-only variant on synthetic tabletop scenes "resembling those used in the evaluation of AIR" that "does not require training" and runs at "0.5s per frame" at low resolution (qualitative comparison only). Benchmark/metric: YCB-Video and a 2000-image synthetic "YCB-Challenging" set under the BOP protocol (RGB-D image plus object types and counts given), ADD-S accuracy at 0.5/1/2 cm thresholds; e.g. Table 1 shows 3DP3 beating DenseFusion on almost all objects (036_wood_block at 0.5 cm: 0.36 vs 0.07; 061_foam_brick: 0.26 vs 0.01). No CLEVR-like quantitative numbers. Relation to ours: same render-and-compare logic and the same trick of enumerating cube-symmetry orientations, but it consumes depth and a known camera, works from one view, and needs a probabilistic-programming runtime; we get an explicit state from two uncalibrated RGB views with deterministic tools sequenced by the LLM.

**Bayes3D (Gothoskar et al. 2023, 2312.08715).** Representation: voxel meshes built from 5-10 RGB-D views with a calibrated camera, scene graph of objects, hierarchical Bayesian depth likelihood with inlier/outlier noise parameters. Inference: GPU-accelerated coarse-to-fine sequential Monte Carlo; 2048 scene hypotheses rendered in parallel with OpenGL and scored in JAX; objects added sequentially. Training: none in the neural sense (object models are "learned" by overlaying point clouds); the paper compares against CNNs trained on synthetic data and shows Bayes3D needs "orders of magnitude less training data". Benchmark/metric: micro-benchmarks (mug orientation posterior via von Mises cross-entropy; hammer-vs-dumbbell identity), 19-object confusion matrices, and camera tracking on synthetic 120-frame sequences — Table I: 103 FPS / 0.130 cm / 1.5 deg at 25x25 px for the mustard bottle, 14.5 FPS / 0.002 cm / 0.74 deg at 200x200. No CLEVR-like results. Relation to ours: the closest "no neural network, real-time inverse graphics" system; the gap is RGB-only input, unknown intrinsics/extrinsics, and multi-object explicit state without a depth sensor.

**CLEVR (Johnson et al. 2016, 1612.06890).** Representation: scenes are sampled scene graphs (3-10 objects; shape in {cube, sphere, cylinder}, two sizes, two materials, eight colours; positions on the ground plane) rendered in Blender with "randomly jittered" light and camera positions; questions are functional programs executed on the scene graph. Inference: none — it is a dataset; six VQA baselines (Q-type mode, LSTM, CNN+BoW, CNN+LSTM, CNN+LSTM+MCB, CNN+LSTM+SA) are trained and evaluated. Metric: question-answering accuracy per question type (overall numbers appear only in Figure 4, a plot, so we do not quote them). Runtime: not applicable. Relation to ours: our room generator is CLEVR-like (primitive shapes, discrete sizes/colours, random lighting) but adds random cube rotations, uses two views, and scores the *state* rather than answers to questions; CLEVR's own GT has no orientation and the authors explicitly note that "a hand-crafted system with explicit knowledge of the CLEVR universe might work well".

**Neural Scene De-rendering (Wu, Tenenbaum, Kohli 2017, 10.1109/cvpr.2017.744).** Representation: "scene XML" — per object category, appearance (size, colour), 3D position {x, y, z} and pose (yaw, pitch, roll) — the closest published output format to our per-object JSON. Inference: segment-proposal encoder predicts each object's XML; a graphics engine (Abstract Scene or Minecraft via Malmo) is the decoder; non-differentiable rendering is handled with multi-sample REINFORCE; an optional 10-iteration Gibbs analysis-by-synthesis refinement (uniform proposals for discrete, Gaussian for continuous variables) is run from the network prediction. Training: required (supervised prediction loss plus reconstruction reward; a semi-supervised curriculum variant). Benchmark/metric: Abstract Scene (10,020 images, 3-18 objects) and a new Minecraft set (10,000 images, 3-5 objects from 12 entity types, positions rounded to 0.1); reported as % of incorrectly inferred representation values and % of wrongly reconstructed pixels, plus a human preference study. Table 1 (as extracted from the PDF; column order inferred from layout): NSD full = 42.74 % inference error / 21.55 % pixel error on Abstract Scene and 26.41 % / 5.05 % on Minecraft, vs. CNN+LSTM 45.31 / 41.38 and 43.52 / 20.22. Runtime: not reported. Relation to ours: same "explicit, renderer-consumable state" goal, but single-view, learned, and its error metric ignores object symmetries and metric distance.

**BOP metric (Hodan et al. 2018, 1808.08319; BOP Challenge 2020, 2009.07378).** BOP 2018 introduced VSD, the Visible Surface Discrepancy, which is computed only over the visible object surface so that "indistinguishable poses are treated as equivalent", in explicit contrast to Hinterstoisser's ADD/ADI. BOP 2020 added two symmetry-aware errors: MSSD (maximum over model vertices of the distance between the estimated pose and the ground-truth pose composed with a symmetry, minimised over the set S of global symmetry transformations) and MSPD (the same in 2D projection). S is built by thresholding the Hausdorff distance between the model in canonical and transformed pose and then keeping only symmetries the texture cannot resolve; continuous symmetries are discretised. A pose is correct if e < theta_e; Recall is the fraction of annotated instances with a correct pose; AR averages recall over thresholds (VSD also over misalignment tolerance), and AR_dataset is the mean of AR_VSD, AR_MSSD, AR_MSPD. In the BOP localisation task the test input includes, for each object, the number of instances present, so estimates are scored per annotated instance and the benchmark does not need an extras penalty (BOP 2024 later introduced a *detection* task where identities are not given).

## (c) What our scorer borrows and what it adds

Borrowed from BOP: the principle that pose error must be minimised over the object's symmetry group before comparison (MSSD/MSPD's min over S), and a threshold/score view of correctness rather than raw distance. Our cube's 24 rotational symmetries are exactly the discrete global symmetry set BOP would build for an untextured cube, and 3DP3 uses the same 24 as its orientation proposal set. Borrowed from CLEVR: the scene-graph-as-ground-truth idea (a list of objects with shape, size, colour, position), the primitive-shape synthetic universe, and per-attribute scoring.

Added beyond both: (1) a *scene-level* symmetry — the two views are uncalibrated and the unit room is itself a cube, so the whole predicted scene may differ from the ground truth by any of the 48 elements of the cube's full symmetry group; our metric minimises over these 48 global transforms (BOP assumes a known camera frame and never needs this; CLEVR fixes one camera). (2) Explicit assignment matching between predicted and ground-truth object sets (BOP's ViVo task hands the method the instance count per object; CLEVR never compares object sets). (3) A penalty for extra predicted objects, which is necessary because, unlike BOP localisation, the number of objects is unknown; this is closer in spirit to BOP 2024's 6D detection task than to the 2018-2020 localisation task. (4) Joint scoring of discrete attributes (shape, colour, size) with continuous pose, in one number, which neither BOP (pose only, identities given) nor CLEVR (question answers only) does. What we do not have relative to BOP: a visible-surface-only error (VSD) — our objects are convex primitives so self-occlusion ambiguity is only the cube rotation, which the 24-fold minimisation already removes.
